# Background & Summary

In the United States, approximately 12.3 million children under the age of five require regular nonparental care arrangements while their parents or guardians work^1^. The cost of this care represents a substantial household burden: the U.S. Department of the Treasury estimated in 2021 that the average family with at least one child under five would need to devote roughly 13 percent of household income to child care, nearly double the 7 percent affordability threshold established by federal guidelines^2^. At the same time, the child care workforce earns among the lowest wages of any occupation in the country, creating persistent staffing shortages that constrain the supply of available care^3^. These affordability and supply pressures do not fall evenly across populations. Lower-income families, rural communities, and communities of color face disproportionate gaps in access to licensed child care, with consequences for maternal labor force participation, household economic stability, and children's developmental trajectories^4,5^.

The geographic distribution of child care supply relative to demand is central to understanding these disparities. Nationally representative surveys such as the National Survey of Early Care and Education (NSECE) and the National Household Education Surveys Program (NHES) provide valuable information on child care utilization patterns and costs at the household level^6,7^. However, these instruments are designed as sample surveys and do not support spatial analysis of provider accessibility at sub-county geographic scales. County-level and state-level aggregations, including those published annually by Child Care Aware of America, document broad trends in supply and affordability but mask substantial within-county variation^8^. Policy and planning decisions about where to site new facilities, how to target subsidies, and which communities face the most acute shortages require spatially resolved data at the neighborhood level^9^.

Several research groups have applied spatial accessibility methods to child care. Malik and Hamm introduced the concept of "child care deserts," defined as census tracts with more than three children under age five for every licensed child care slot, and found that more than half of Americans live in such areas^10^. Morrissey, Allard, and Pelletier examined county-level variation in early care and education access, finding that rural children have lower access to private center-based programs and depend more heavily on public options such as Head Start^11^. Blumenberg, Yao, and Wander applied the two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method to examine variation in child care access across different neighborhood types, demonstrating that spatial accessibility measures reveal disparities not captured by simple supply counts^12^. In a related study, Blumenberg, Wander, and Yao found that greater spatial accessibility to formal child care, as measured by a 2SFCA ratio, is associated with increased utilization and reduced travel distances^13^. Azuma and colleagues developed a micro-level affordability indicator using a 2SFCA method within three-mile driving distances in Honolulu County, finding that only 14 percent of young children had access to affordable early care and education programs meeting federal cost thresholds^14^. Herbst and Tekin exploited geographic variation in distance to social service agencies as a proxy for child care subsidy accessibility, linking subsidy receipt to childhood health outcomes, but relied on distance to administrative offices rather than to actual child care providers^15^. Pennerstorfer and Pennerstorfer applied spatial accessibility methods to child care provision in Vienna and found that accessibility is highest in the highest socioeconomic status neighborhoods, with inequality increasing over time^16^.

While these studies demonstrate the analytical value of spatially resolved child care accessibility measures, none has produced a publicly available, regularly updated, multi-measure dataset at the census block group level for any U.S. state. The individual analyses were conducted for specific research questions in specific geographies and were not designed for reuse as open data products. The floating catchment area family of methods, originally developed for measuring spatial access to health services^17,18^, has been extended through the three-step floating catchment area (3SFCA) method, which introduces a selection-weight normalization step to account for competition among consumers for nearby providers^19^. The 3SFCA method addresses a well-documented overestimation problem in the two-step formulation, where populations near multiple providers are counted as full demand at each facility. This correction is particularly relevant for child care markets, where parents exercise choice among providers based on distance, capacity, age eligibility, and cost, and where supply in one location partially satisfies demand in neighboring areas.

The dataset described in this Data Descriptor provides five measures of licensed child care accessibility for the Commonwealth of Virginia at the census block group level for the years 2021 and 2025. The measures are: (1) total licensed child care capacity, defined as the number of licensed seats within each block group; (2) drive time to the nearest licensed child care provider, calculated using the Open Source Routing Machine (OSRM) from block group centroids; and (3-5) three age-stratified 3SFCA accessibility ratios expressing licensed child care seats per 1,000 children, computed for children under 15 (providers accepting ages 4 to 10), children ages 5 to 14 (providers with minimum accepted age over 4), and children under 10 (providers with maximum accepted age under 10). Provider data were obtained by scraping the Virginia Department of Social Services (VDSS) licensed child day care facility search portal, which includes facility name, address, licensed capacity, and accepted age range. Provider locations were geocoded using the U.S. Census Bureau geocoder and assigned to the nearest block group centroid via haversine distance. Child population denominators were drawn from the American Community Survey (ACS) five-year estimates, Table B01001 (Sex by Age). Travel times between block group centroids were derived from pre-computed OSRM routing matrices covering Virginia and its bordering states (Maryland, the District of Columbia, Delaware, Kentucky, North Carolina, Tennessee, and West Virginia) to account for cross-border travel. Block group measures were aggregated to census tract, county, and Virginia health district levels using population-weighted averages for ratios, arithmetic means for drive times, and sums for capacity.

The resulting dataset supports several categories of reuse. Public health departments and planning agencies can use the block-group-level accessibility ratios to identify neighborhoods with insufficient licensed child care supply relative to the resident child population. Researchers studying the relationship between child care access and outcomes such as maternal employment or school readiness can use these measures as spatially resolved independent variables at multiple geographic scales^4,20^. The inclusion of two time points (2021 and 2025) supports analysis of how the distribution of child care supply changed in the period following the COVID-19 pandemic, during which the child care sector experienced significant workforce losses and facility closures^3^. The dataset is deposited on Zenodo and is freely available for download. All code used to produce the dataset is published in a public repository, enabling full reproducibility and adaptation to other states or time periods.
